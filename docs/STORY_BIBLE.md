# Story bible and sample scripts

## Setting

Queue City is a fictional coastal town where every problem arrives in a paper envelope, even when it started online. The municipal queue-ticket machine, **No. 404**, has acquired wheels and a strong opinion about deadlines. It comments on events but never gives legal advice.

The town has four districts: Lantern Market, Ferry Quay, Workshop Row, and North Steps. No district is merely a level. People cross between them, and their choices travel too.

## Tone rules

- Jokes target bureaucracy, absurd systems, and the player's own grand plans; people needing help are never the punchline.
- A document can be funny without making a real rule false.
- NPCs have agency. They remember help, delay, honesty, and broken promises.
- Every major scene gives the player at least two defensible strategies with a real cost.

## Opening script

**No. 404:** “Good morning. Your ticket is number 404. This is not an error. I have chosen it.”

**Veera:** “The market opens Friday, the ferry is late, and someone has printed three different versions of the same notice. We have four weeks to stop a small problem from becoming the city's personality.”

**Player role introduction:**

- **Advice lead:** “Your desk has five empty chairs, one working pen, and exactly enough time to disappoint someone.”
- **Mayor:** “The emergency budget is technically yours. So is every complaint about how you spend it.”
- **Co-op founder:** “Your members will help anyone. Your bank account has requested a second opinion.”

## Sample scene: the market invoice

**Nelli:** “I sell soup. Ilo sells cleaning kits. The poster says one price label for the whole market. Is the poster right?”

**Miro:** “If the labels change after I print them, I am moving to the ferry.”

The player can spend time verifying the categories and communicating clearly, rush out one convenient sign, or postpone the launch to gain certainty. A role-specific approach may fund a clerk, convene volunteers, or open a source clinic. The source card points to Vero's current VAT rates page; the fictional market consequences are game writing.

## Sample scene: the late envelope

**Safa:** “I don't need a heroic promise. I need to know which document is missing.”

**Otso:** “We can delay payroll for a day. Not a week.”

The choice is about time allocation and honest uncertainty. A quick answer might ease this week's queue but increase the chance of a later correction. A document check protects Safa while consuming capacity another NPC hoped to use.

## Sample scene: the poster war

**Kaiku:** “Three posters, one rule, and four hundred opinions. Which sentence do I put on the radio?”

**No. 404:** “For balance, I have prepared a fifth poster. It is blank.”

The player can fund a public explainer, offer individual clinics, or stay quiet while focusing on urgent cases. Each helps a different part of the NPC network.

## Endings

Endings combine city resources, resolved story flags, and role. An ending should name at least three NPC outcomes and make the tradeoff legible. There is no single canonical 'good' ending. Failure means the city suffers a concrete loss; it is not a moral verdict on the player.
